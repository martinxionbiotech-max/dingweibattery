# Battery shipping, packaging and warranty

Published by Dingwei Battery · Last reviewed 2026-09-10 · Sources: IMO IMDG Code, US DOT PHMSA, export logistics practice

A battery order is not complete when it leaves the line — it is complete when it arrives undamaged, with the right documents, and with a warranty you understand. This page covers the three parts that decide whether a shipment actually delivers its value: classification, packaging, and warranty scope.

**Short answer:** Lead-acid batteries are Class 8 (corrosive) dangerous goods — UN 2794 filled with acid, or UN 2800 non-spillable — but, unlike lithium, they do not require a UN 38.3 report. Packaging must keep terminals from shorting and the case from impact, the export set needs at least five documents, and a warranty is only as good as its written duration, exclusions and claim handling.

## Dangerous-goods classification

Lead-acid batteries are not general cargo. The electrolyte is sulfuric acid, so the product is Class 8 corrosive under the \[IMO IMDG Code\](https://www.imo.org/), which governs sea freight, with the UN numbers themselves set by the \[UN Model Regulations\](https://unece.org/transport/dangerous-goods). Flooded batteries ship as **UN 2794 — Batteries, wet, filled with acid**; sealed, non-spillable designs that pass vibration and pressure-differential tests ship as **UN 2800 — Batteries, wet, non-spillable**. The full logic — including the UN 2800 test requirements and the comparison with lithium's UN 3480/UN 3481 — is in the [dangerous-goods classification](/knowledge/lead-acid-battery-dangerous-goods/) page.

## Packaging and container loading

Batteries are dense, so the loading math works backwards: a standard 20ft container offers about 33 m³ of volume but only about 28,000 kg of payload, and a battery load hits the weight ceiling before the volume. That is why MOQ follows the container (typically 1 × 20ft container (approx. 600–800 units, model dependent)). Packaging has three jobs:

-   **Terminal protection** — prevent short circuits between adjacent batteries.
-   **Impact protection** — keep the case intact through handling and container movement.
-   **Upright orientation** — keep flooded batteries upright so acid stays in the cells.

Pallets are usually kept to about 1.5 m height and 1.5 tonnes for safe stacking, and mixed-model loading spreads unit cost across a full container. The [MOQ and lead time](/knowledge/oem-battery-moq-lead-time/) page explains the weight logic in detail.

## Export documentation

A clean export set avoids delays at customs. The baseline five documents:

-   Commercial invoice and packing list
-   Bill of lading
-   Certificate of origin
-   Dangerous-goods declaration (the one amateurs miss)
-   Test reports (CCA / capacity) and MSDS where required

Shipping terms follow FOB Tianjin / CFR / CIF, with export from Tianjin / Shanghai. Each document and what it proves is in the [export documentation](/knowledge/battery-export-documentation/) page.

## Warranty: read the scope, not just the number

Warranty is 12 months from bill of lading date. But a warranty is only as good as its written scope. Confirm three things before relying on it:

1.  **Duration** — from bill of lading date, not from your customer's purchase.
2.  **Exclusions** — what is _not_ covered: deep discharge, misuse, physical damage, poor charging.
3.  **Handling** — how a claim is filed, inspected and settled, and whether settlement is full replacement or pro-rata.

A “12-month warranty” with broad exclusions is not the same as a 12-month warranty with clear, narrow ones — and a pro-rata settlement is worth less than a full replacement. Get the terms in writing.

The claim-side detail — how a failure is judged, how credit is calculated and how long it takes — is in the [warranty claim process](/knowledge/battery-warranty-claim-process/) page.

## Storage: the warranty you lose silently

Packaging and warranty both assume the battery ships soon after production. A battery that sits in a warehouse is still discharging — lead-acid self-discharges at roughly 3–5% per month, and below about 50% charge sulfation begins to harden on the plates. Store cool and fully charged, and top up every 6 months. The [storage and shelf life](/knowledge/battery-storage-shelf-life/) page turns this into a warehouse procedure.

## What buyers should ask

-   How are batteries packed and loaded — is the container weight-limited or volume-limited?
-   Which export documents are provided as standard, and who signs the dangerous-goods declaration?
-   What are the exact warranty exclusions and claim handling — full replacement or pro-rata?
-   What Incoterm applies, and where does risk transfer?
