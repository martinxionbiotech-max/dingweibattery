# OEM battery cost breakdown

Published by Dingwei Battery · Last reviewed 2026-09-10 · Sources: IMARC lead-acid manufacturing cost report, LME lead price data

A lead-acid battery's cost is not a mystery — it is mostly lead. The structure is commodity-dominated, which means two things for a buyer: the price moves with the LME, and a quote that is dramatically cheaper than its peers is almost always a smaller scope, not a better factory. This page breaks the cost down line by line and gives you the arithmetic to test any quote.

**Short answer:** Lead-acid battery cost is dominated by raw materials at 75–80% of operating cost, with lead as the single largest input, utilities at 10–15%, and the balance in labour, packaging, transport and overhead. A 15 kg starter battery carries roughly 9–13 kg of lead — about US$20–25 at an LME price near US$1,950/tonne — so your battery's price moves with the lead market, and a low quote usually hides a smaller scope.

## The cost structure, line by line

Industry cost modelling (see the \[IMARC lead-acid manufacturing report\](https://www.imarcgroup.com/lead-acid-battery-manufacturing-plant-project-report)) puts the operating-cost structure roughly as follows:

| Cost category | Share of operating cost | What is inside it |
|---|---|---|
| Raw materials | 75–80% | Lead (grids + oxide), sulfuric acid, polypropylene (case + separator) |
| Utilities | 10–15% | Electricity for formation and casting, water, compressed air |
| Labour + overhead | 5–10% | Direct labour, packaging, transport, maintenance, depreciation, taxes |

Lead is the swing factor. It trades on the London Metal Exchange at around US$1,950–1,960 per tonne through 2025–26 (see \[Investing News Network's lead forecast\](https://investingnews.com/lead-forecast)), and because lead-acid is the largest single use of lead, the battery's ex-factory price tracks that number closely.

## A worked unit-cost example

Here is the arithmetic for a typical 12V 60–72 Ah SLI starter battery, using the cost structure above. The numbers are illustrative — they show the logic, not a quote.

| Line | Value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Battery weight | ~15 kg | Typical JIS/DIN D23–D26 class |
| Lead content | ~9–13 kg | Roughly 60–70% of battery mass |
| Lead value | ~US$20–25 | 9–13 kg × ~US$1.95/kg (LME) |
| Raw-material total | ~75–80% of cost | IMARC structure |
| Utilities + labour + overhead | ~20–25% of cost | Remainder of the structure |

The lesson is the **lead-linkage test**: lead alone is the single largest cost line, and it is set by a commodity exchange, not by the factory. Two factories quoting 10% apart on the same specification are usually quoting different lead-purchase timing, different margin, or different scope — not different quality. ## Where the non-material costs hide

Beyond the commodity, four cost lines separate a real program from a cheap one, and each can be silently omitted from a low quote:

-   **Certification** — IATF 16949 and ISO 9001 are factory-level overheads spread across all output, but per-market conformity (CE, SASO/GSO for the Gulf, destination test reports) is a per-program cost plus audit time.
-   **Tooling** — a custom case, lid or terminal layout carries a one-time mould cost, usually amortised across the first order and sometimes waived at volume.
-   **Packaging** — export cartons, pallets and terminal protection are priced per unit and can vary widely between "domestic" and "export-grade" packing.
-   **Logistics** — a 20ft container of batteries ships at FOB Tianjin / CFR / CIF, and the freight per unit collapses at a full container load (1 × 20ft container (approx. 600–800 units, model dependent)).

## The hidden costs in a low quote

A quote that looks 10–15% cheaper is usually a smaller scope, dressed as a better price. The four things that hide inside a suspiciously low number:

1.  **The CCA test basis** — JIS (−15°C) and EN (−18°C) produce different numbers; a quote without the basis is quoting the easier test.
2.  **The trading-company margin** — a trader fronting as a factory adds 5–15% and removes your quality visibility.
3.  **Tooling not amortised** — a quote that excludes the mould cost just moves it to the second order.
4.  **Domestic packaging** — a battery that survives a local truck may not survive a sea container without export-grade cartons.

The rule is simple: compare on scope, not on the bare number. A complete scope — CCA basis, certification, tooling, packaging, Incoterm — is the only basis on which two quotes mean the same thing.

## Why the recycled value matters

One cost line works in your favour: lead is recyclable, and a spent lead battery has scrap value. The lead industry reports an end-of-life net credit of roughly US$33 per kWh for lead batteries, versus about US$91 per kWh of cost for lithium (see \[Battery Council International's lead battery facts\](https://batterycouncil.org/sources)). For a B2B buyer the scrap credit usually sits with the recycler, not you — but it is part of why lead-acid remains economically rational even as lithium grows, and it is worth knowing when a customer asks about total cost of ownership.
