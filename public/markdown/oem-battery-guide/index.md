# OEM battery manufacturing: the complete guide

Published by Dingwei Battery · Last reviewed 2026-09-10 · Sources: IATF 16949, IMARC lead-acid manufacturing cost report, LME lead price data

OEM battery manufacturing is not buying a product — it is commissioning a repeatable supply arrangement around your specification and your brand. This guide walks the whole thing in order: what OEM actually means, how a program is structured, the seven-stage development process, where the cost goes, and how OEM compares with aftermarket supply. Read it once before you write an RFQ, and you will ask for the right things in the right order.

**Short answer:** OEM battery manufacturing means a factory builds batteries to your specification — voltage, Ah, CCA basis, terminal, label — under your brand. A program runs through specification, sampling, production and locked repeat orders, on a 60–90 day timeline, and the dominant cost is lead: raw materials are 75–80% of operating cost. The quality is set by the specification you sign, not by the label, so the entire skill of OEM is writing a specification that cannot drift and a contract that enforces it.

## What OEM means in the battery trade

OEM — original equipment manufacturer — is one of the most misused words in sourcing, because it means different things in different industries. In automotive parts the OEM is usually the car maker. In battery export, the word is used the other way round: _you_ are the buyer who owns the design and the brand, and the factory is the original equipment manufacturer that produces to your specification.

The distinction that matters is ownership. Under an OEM arrangement you bring the specification; the factory brings the production capability. Under ODM the factory brings a ready design you can brand and adjust. Under private label you put your identity on a supplied platform. All three can look identical on a shelf; the difference is who owns the design, who carries the development risk, and how locked the repeat order is. If this is new ground, start with the [what is an OEM battery](/knowledge/what-is-oem-battery/) explainer and the [OEM vs ODM vs private label](/knowledge/oem-vs-odm-vs-private-label/) decision guide before reading on.

## The five decisions that define an OEM program

Before anything is built, five parameters have to be fixed — together, not one at a time. A factory that quotes "a 72 Ah battery" without asking the rest is guessing, and so is its price.

1.  **Standard family** — JIS, DIN-EN, BCI, AS or SA. This sets the case, the CCA test method and the market the battery is legal to sell into.
2.  **Case group and size** — D26, D23, B24, L3, H6 or a custom footprint. This determines what vehicles the battery fits.
3.  **Electrical specification** — voltage, capacity at C20 (Ah), reserve capacity, and CCA on a named test basis (JIS −15°C, EN −18°C, SAE −18°C). The basis matters: a JIS CCA and an EN CCA are not directly comparable.
4.  **Terminal and polarity** — type, position and orientation, because a correct battery with the wrong terminal is a returns line, not a sale.
5.  **Technology** — SLI, EFB, AGM or heavy-duty, which sets the plate design, the separator and the price band.

The full treatment of each parameter lives in the specifications cluster — begin with the [automotive battery specifications reference](/knowledge/battery-specifications-guide/) and lock the footprint against the [group size chart](/knowledge/battery-group-size-chart/).

## How an OEM program is structured

An OEM program is a repeatable supply arrangement, not a one-off order. It runs in four phases, and the point of the structure is that the tenth container matches the first approved sample:

| Phase | What is produced | Your checkpoint |
|---|---|---|
| 1\. Specification | Written spec sheet | Sign every parameter, including the CCA basis |
| 2\. Sampling | Pre-production sample + test report | Test the physical sample, not just the drawing |
| 3\. Production | Container-load batches | Confirm schedule and change freeze |
| 4\. Repeat orders | Identical follow-on batches | Re-check against the approved sample |

The mechanism that makes the repeat order safe is the **specification lock** — the approved sample plus written parameters plus change control. Read the [how OEM programs work](/knowledge/how-oem-battery-programs-work/) page for the four phases in detail and the [development process](/knowledge/oem-battery-development-process/) page for the seven stages and where you sign off.

## The seven-stage development process

From idea to shipped container, an OEM battery project passes seven gates. Each gate is a decision point where the buyer either signs off or the specification drifts:

1.  **Consultation** — feasibility against vehicle, market and volume.
2.  **Specification** — the written spec sheet, signed line by line.
3.  **Design and branding** — label, case and packaging artwork.
4.  **Sampling** — a pre-production sample from a production-equivalent line.
5.  **Mass production** — scheduled manufacture with in-process control.
6.  **Quality inspection** — test data against agreed acceptance criteria.
7.  **Shipping** — export packaging and documentation.

Four of those seven gates need a buyer signature, not just a factory confirmation: specification, sample, quality standard and shipment. Skip one and the next order can come back different from what you approved. The [development process](/knowledge/oem-battery-development-process/) page walks each stage with the deliverable and the checkpoint.

## Where the cost goes

Lead-acid batteries are a commodity-plus business: the cost is dominated by materials, and the material that swings the price is lead. The industry cost structure runs roughly like this — raw materials at 75–80% of operating cost, utilities at 10–15%, and the balance in labour, packaging, transport, maintenance and depreciation, per IMARC's manufacturing cost modelling (see the \[IMARC lead-acid manufacturing report\](https://www.imarcgroup.com/lead-acid-battery-manufacturing-plant-project-report)).

Lead (grids plus oxide paste), sulfuric acid and polypropylene are the three main material inputs, and lead is the largest by a wide margin. Because lead trades on the LME — around US$1,950–1,960 per tonne through 2025–26 (see \[Investing News Network's lead forecast\](https://investingnews.com/lead-forecast)) — your battery's price moves with the LME, not with the factory's mood. A factory that quotes a fixed price for 12 months is either hedging lead or carrying the risk for you, and you should know which.

The full arithmetic — including a worked unit-cost example and the hidden costs that hide inside a low quote — is in the [OEM cost breakdown](/knowledge/oem-battery-cost-breakdown/) page.

## OEM versus aftermarket: what you are actually buying

The OEM/aftermarket question is really three separate questions bundled into one: warranty coverage, real-world lifespan and production consistency. OEM supply from the original factory gives you specification control and consistency across containers; aftermarket supply gives you price and availability from stock. The trade-off is not "good versus cheap" — it is "control versus convenience." A full side-by-side, including the warranty and consistency dimensions that decide repeat business, is in the [OEM vs aftermarket](/knowledge/oem-battery-vs-aftermarket/) page.

## Two numbers that reframe an OEM decision

**The lead-linkage test.** If raw materials are 75–80% of cost and lead is the dominant material, then a 10% move in the LME lead price moves your ex-factory price by a material percentage. At roughly US$1,950/tonne, a single 15 kg SLI battery contains on the order of US$20–25 of lead. That is why two factories quoting 10% apart on the same specification are usually quoting different lead-purchase timing or different margin, not different quality — and why a fixed-price clause is a negotiation point, not a formality.

**The specification-drift cost.** The expensive mistake in OEM is not a bad first order — it is a first order you cannot repeat. A skipped sampling gate or an unsigned CCA basis can make every later container a new development project, each carrying its own approval risk. The cheapest line item in the whole program is the one that prevents that: a documented sample approval and a locked specification.

## What suppliers will not volunteer

-   **A low unit price hides a smaller scope.** A quote that omits the CCA test basis, certification, tooling or packaging is not a cheaper battery — it is a cheaper specification. Compare on scope.
-   **"Same as sample" is not a warranty.** Unless the golden sample is referenced in the contract with its test report, "same as sample" means nothing a court can enforce.
-   **Lead price moves are passed on eventually.** A factory absorbing lead swings to win your order will recover them somewhere — in margin on tooling, in freight, or on the second container.
-   **MOQ follows the container, not your demand.** A 20ft container holds roughly 600–800 starter batteries because weight caps the load before volume does — so a "minimum order" is a logistics fact, not a marketing preference.

## The 60–90 day timeline

The whole cycle from first RFQ to landed goods breaks into a predictable calendar:

| Weeks | Stage | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Specify + RFQ | Lock the 5-part specification; issue one RFQ on one scope. |
| 2–4 | Sample + approve | Produce and test the pre-production sample; sign it off. |
| 4–10 | Production | 30–45 days of casting through formation, sealing and test. |
| 10–14 | Shipping + clearance | 3–5 weeks of ocean freight and customs. |

The two places the schedule slips are sampling (because the specification was not locked first) and clearance (because a certification was discovered late). Both are preventable at the front of the sequence.

## Reading an OEM quote: the six lines that decide the price

Two factories can quote the "same battery" and be US$8 apart, and the difference is almost never raw profit — it is scope. When you hold two quotes side by side, compare these six lines before you compare the total:

1.  **The CCA test basis** — JIS (−15°C) and EN (−18°C) produce different numbers for the same battery. A quote that does not name the basis is quoting the easier test.
2.  **The case and lid source** — a custom mould is a one-time tooling cost; a stock case is not. Know which you are buying.
3.  **The technology spec** — SLI, EFB and AGM are three different products, not three price tiers of one product.
4.  **Certification included** — IATF 16949 is a factory-level given; per-market conformity (CE, SASO/GSO) is a line item. Confirm which are in the price.
5.  **Packaging grade** — domestic cartons and export-grade cartons cost differently and survive differently.
6.  **The Incoterm** — FOB, CFR and CIF move the freight and insurance cost between the two of you.

The discipline is simple and mechanical: a quote is comparable only when all six lines are answered on the same basis. Send one RFQ on one scope, and the price spread that remains is real negotiation, not scope illusion. The [RFQ guide](/knowledge/oem-battery-rfq-guide/) has the template.

## A worked program example: one container a month

To make the economics concrete, take a buyer running one 20ft container a month for 24 months. At roughly 700 batteries a container and a landed cost near US$40 a unit, each container is about US$28,000, and the two-year program is around US$670,000. Now place the costs that decide whether the program survives:

| Line | Cost | What it buys |
|---|---|---|
| Factory audit (one-time) | US$1,500–3,000 | Confirmation the supplier can repeat your spec |
| Sampling + approval (one-time) | 10–20 days | The golden sample that anchors every later batch |
| Specification drift (if skipped) | One ruined container ≈ US$28,000 | The failure the first two lines prevent |

The audit is under 0.5% of the program's value, and the sampling window is the cheapest insurance line in it. The reason to run a program rather than a string of orders is exactly this arithmetic: the one-time setup costs are amortised to nothing across 24 containers, while the drift risk they retire is present on every single one.

## The terms that get misused

Five terms carry most of the confusion in OEM battery sourcing, so it is worth pinning them down:

-   **OEM** — you bring the specification; the factory builds to it. You own design and brand.
-   **ODM** — the factory brings the design; you brand and adjust it. They own the design.
-   **White label** — you label an off-the-shelf product a competitor can also buy.
-   **Golden sample** — the approved pre-production sample every later batch is checked against.
-   **Specification lock** — the sample plus written parameters plus change control that makes repeat orders consistent.

Ownership is the thread through all five. The deeper your ownership of the design, the more control and the more development work — and the more the product is yours alone.
