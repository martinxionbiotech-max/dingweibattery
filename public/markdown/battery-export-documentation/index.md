# Battery export documentation

Published by Dingwei Battery · Last reviewed 2026-09-10 · Sources: IMO IMDG Code, export logistics practice, Incoterms 2020

A battery container moves on documents, not just on a ship. The paperwork set is what keeps a Class 8 load flowing through customs instead of sitting in a bond racking up demurrage. This page lists each document, what it proves, and the one most exporters forget.

**Short answer:** A lead-acid battery export needs at least five documents — commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, certificate of origin and a dangerous-goods declaration — plus a CCA/capacity test report and MSDS where the destination or carrier requires them. The dangerous-goods declaration is the one amateurs miss, and it must be signed by a certified person because the battery ships as Class 8 corrosive.

## The five-document baseline

| Document | What it proves | Who issues it |
|---|---|---|
| Commercial invoice | Value, terms, buyer and seller | Exporter |
| Packing list | Carton counts, weights, dimensions per pallet | Exporter |
| Bill of lading | Receipt and contract of carriage | Carrier |
| Certificate of origin | Where the goods were manufactured | Chamber of commerce |
| Dangerous-goods declaration | UN number, class and packing, signed | Certified shipper |

## The dangerous-goods declaration, in detail

This is the document that separates a professional export from an amateur one. Because lead-acid batteries are Class 8 corrosive under the \[IMDG Code\](https://www.imo.org/), the shipper must declare the UN number (UN 2794 or UN 2800), the proper shipping name, the class and the packing — and the declaration must be signed by a person certified in dangerous-goods regulations, under the rules set by the \[UN Model Regulations\](https://unece.org/transport/dangerous-goods). A battery shipped as "general merchandise" is a misdeclaration, and it is the most common hazmat violation in the industry. The full classification logic is in the [dangerous-goods classification](/knowledge/lead-acid-battery-dangerous-goods/) page.

## Supporting documents that travel with the load

-   **CCA / capacity test report** — proof the battery meets its labelled specification; requested by many destinations and embedded in most OEM contracts.
-   **MSDS (material safety data sheet)** — the safety data for the electrolyte and the battery; required by carriers and customs in most markets.
-   **Test-report / conformity certificates** — CE for Europe, SASO/GSO for the Gulf, or destination-specific conformity, where the market demands them.
-   **Insurance certificate** — evidence of cargo cover when the buyer requires it, especially under CIF.

The destination-specific requirements are mapped market by market in the [import regulations](/knowledge/battery-import-regulations/) page.

## How Incoterms divide the work

The Incoterm decides who arranges and pays for each document and each carriage leg. Dingwei Battery ships on FOB Tianjin / CFR / CIF:

-   **FOB** (Free On Board) — the seller delivers to the port and clears export; the buyer takes risk and freight from the port onward.
-   **CFR** (Cost and Freight) — the seller also pays freight to the destination port, but risk still transfers at the origin port.
-   **CIF** (Cost, Insurance and Freight) — the seller adds insurance to the destination port; the buyer handles import clearance.

The practical point: under FOB the buyer owns the clearance documents and the freight; under CIF the seller arranges more of it. Choose the term that puts the documentation in the hands of whoever has the local relationships to clear it.

## What slows a battery shipment down

-   **A missing or unsigned dangerous-goods declaration** — the single most common hold.
-   **A certificate of origin that does not match the invoice** — discrepancies between documents trigger customs queries.
-   **Test reports for a different model than the shipment** — the report must match the actual batteries on the pallet.
-   **Weights that do not reconcile** — the packing list, invoice and bill of lading must agree to the kilogram.

## The three discrepancies that hold a shipment

Most battery customs delays are not missing documents — they are documents that disagree with each other. Three mismatches cause the bulk of holds:

1.  **Invoice vs packing list weights** — a kilogram difference between the two triggers a query. Reconcile to the kilo before dispatch.
2.  **Test report vs actual model** — a CCA/capacity report for a different model than the batteries on the pallet invalidates the compliance claim.
3.  **Certificate of origin vs invoice** — a mismatch between the origin country and the shipper's details raises a customs flag.

The rule is mechanical: one shipment, one consistent document set. Every number on the invoice must match the packing list and the bill of lading, and every test report must match the physical batteries. A 20-minute reconciliation before dispatch costs nothing; the same reconciliation at the destination border costs demurrage and a bond.

## Who signs what

Documentation is not just paper — it is accountability, and each document carries a different signer:

-   **Exporter** — commercial invoice, packing list.
-   **Carrier** — bill of lading.
-   **Chamber of commerce** — certificate of origin.
-   **Certified dangerous-goods person** — the dangerous-goods declaration.

The dangerous-goods declaration is the one that requires a trained and certified signer, which is why it is also the one that separates a professional export from an amateur one.
