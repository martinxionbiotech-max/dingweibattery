# OEM battery MOQ & lead time

Published by Dingwei Battery · Last reviewed 2026-09-10 · Source: Chengguang Power Tech Co., Ltd. technical documentation; export logistics practice

MOQ and lead time are set by production economics and container logistics, not by negotiation alone. Here is the arithmetic behind both, and how to move them in your favour.

**Short answer:** OEM battery MOQ follows the 20ft container — about 33 m³ and a 28,000 kg payload — but pallet stacking caps the practical load at roughly 600–800 units long before either limit binds, which is why MOQ is a container-logic number. A 30–45 day lead time breaks into 3–5 days confirmation, 20–30 days production and 2–3 days pre-shipment inspection, with 3–5 weeks of freight on top.

## What drives MOQ

Battery MOQ is tied to a container load because shipping cost per unit collapses at FCL. Chengguang Power Tech Co., Ltd.’s reference MOQ is 1 × 20ft container (approx. 600–800 units, model dependent).

## The container math, done honestly

Why 600–800 units and not 1,400? Run the three limits:

| Limit | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | 20ft container ≈ 33 m³ ÷ ~0.011 m³ per boxed battery | ≈ 3,000 units |
| Payload | ≈ 28,000 kg ÷ ~19 kg per battery | ≈ 1,470 units |
| Stacking / handling | Pallet load limit and carton crush strength cap the practical load | ≈ 600–800 units |

The honest conclusion: for starter batteries the binding constraint is neither raw volume nor raw weight — it is pallet stacking and carton crush strength. A pallet holding 70 batteries at 19 kg each already weighs about 1,330 kg, near the practical handling limit, and you cannot stack cartons indefinitely without crushing the bottom layer. That is why the “weight before volume” intuition is right at the pallet level even though the container itself could carry more. The MOQ is not a marketing number — it is the safe, economical load.

## What drives lead time

Lead time covers order confirmation, production and pre-shipment testing. Chengguang Power Tech Co., Ltd.’s reference lead time is 30–45 days after order confirmation; custom-label orders add sampling time. Here is the breakdown:

| Stage | Typical days | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| Order confirmation | 3–5 | Specification lock, deposit, sampling approval |
| Production | 20–30 | Casting through formation and sealing |
| Pre-shipment inspection | 2–3 | Final test, packaging check, loading |
| Ocean freight + clearance | 3–5 weeks | Transit, customs and inland delivery (outside the 30–45 day figure) |

The quoted 30–45 days is production lead time, not door-to-door. Freight and clearance add 3–5 weeks on top — a distinction buyers miss when they promise a launch date to a customer.

## How to reduce both

-   Order in full container multiples — FCL pricing is the single biggest unit-cost lever.
-   Consolidate mixed models into one container — spreads the fixed freight across more line items.
-   Confirm specification early — sampling delays are the most avoidable lead-time cost.
-   Plan around the harvest-free, year-round production of lead-acid batteries.

## Why lead-acid has no seasonal cycle

Lead-acid production is not tied to a harvest or seasonal raw-material window. The manufacturing line can run through the year, which keeps lead time more stable than seasonal categories. For an importer, that means a confirmed production lead time is a planning variable, not a harvest risk — and the calendar is something you control, not something you wait on.

## MOQ reflects inventory pressure, not just freight

Here is the part suppliers do not volunteer: MOQ is not only about your freight economics — it is also about the supplier’s inventory. A factory pushing a very high MOQ may be clearing stock or batching production for its own efficiency, not protecting your unit cost. Ask for the MOQ _logic_ — container terms, pallet load, and the model mix — rather than accepting a bare number. A supplier that explains the container math is protecting your cost; one that just names a number may be protecting its own.
