# Battery import regulations by market

Published by Dingwei Battery · Last reviewed 2026-09-10 · Sources: USTR Section 301, EU Regulation (EU) 2023/1542, IMDG Code, HTS

The cheapest battery in the world can become the most expensive one after it clears customs. Import compliance is the stage most buyers discover last, and it is the stage that turns a profitable order into a loss. Know the tariff, the certification and the environmental rules for your destination before you commit a cent.

**Short answer:** Import rules differ by market, and three of them matter most: the tariff (the US adds a 25% Section 301 surcharge on Chinese batteries on top of base duty), the certification (the EU applies Regulation (EU) 2023/1542 and CE marking; Gulf states require SASO/GSO conformity), and the shipping class (lead-acid is Class 8 corrosive dangerous goods, UN 2794 / UN 2800 under the IMDG Code). Estimate landed cost as FOB + freight + insurance + duty + VAT, and remember a 25% surcharge multiplies every earlier line.

## The landed-cost formula

Before the market map, the arithmetic. Landed cost is five lines, added in order:

Landed cost = FOB unit cost + ocean freight + insurance + import duty + destination VAT

Worked example, one battery:

| Line | Amount | Note |
|---|---|---|
| FOB unit cost | US$32.00 | Ex-factory, loaded at port |
| Ocean freight | \+ US$1.50 | 700 units on a 20ft container, freight spread per unit |
| Insurance | \+ US$0.30 | Roughly 1% of insured value |
| Import duty (base + 25% surcharge) | \+ US$9.60 | If base is ~5% and Section 301 adds 25%, about 30% on the duty base |
| Destination VAT | \+ US$8.60 | ~20% on the landed-plus-duty value |

The illustration uses rounded, placeholder rates to show the method — actual duty and VAT depend on the destination country and the current Harmonized Tariff Schedule. The point is structural: a 25% surcharge applied after freight and insurance multiplies every earlier line, so the landed cost is never the FOB price plus a flat 25%.

## United States

Chinese lead-acid batteries are subject to the [Section 301](https://ustr.gov/issue-areas/enforcement/section-301-investigations/) tariffs, which add 25% on top of the base duty for lead-acid starter batteries (HS 8507.10). Check the current rate against the [Harmonized Tariff Schedule](https://hts.usitc.gov/) before quoting — tariff levels change, and a landed-cost model built on the wrong rate is worse than no model. Separate from the general duty, the US has also raised tariffs on Chinese electric-vehicle batteries specifically, but lead-acid starter batteries are their own HS heading and should be looked up individually.

## European Union

The EU applies the [Batteries Regulation (EU) 2023/1542](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/1542/oj), in force since 17 August 2023, which replaced Directive 2006/66/EC. It brings labelling, conformity (CE marking), and extended producer responsibility requirements, and it phases in requirements such as recycled-content reporting and a battery passport for certain categories. For an OEM buyer the practical points are: CE marking where applicable, compliance with the heavy-metal restrictions (lead is permitted but restricted in concentration), and registration/EPR obligations that vary by member state. An importer should confirm the exact conformity route for lead-acid starter batteries with its notified body rather than assume automotive batteries are exempt.

## Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC)

Saudi Arabia and the wider GCC apply conformity programmes — SASO in Saudi Arabia and GSO across the Gulf — requiring third-party conformity assessment and product registration before import. A buyer shipping into the Gulf should confirm the specific technical regulation and the conformity certificate early, because the registration lead time can exceed the production lead time if left late.

## Southeast Asia

Most Southeast Asian markets accept IEC-based testing and local import licensing; some (such as Indonesia with SNI, and the Philippines with BPS/ICC) require a local mark or certificate for certain product categories. The pattern is the same as the Gulf: confirm the local mark requirement before production, because post-arrival certification is expensive and slow.

## Africa

African markets vary widely, from largely open import regimes to mandatory conformity programmes such as Nigeria’s SONCAP and Kenya’s KEBS PVoC (pre-export verification of conformity). The practical rule is to confirm whether the destination runs a pre-export verification programme, because those require certification at origin — which the exporter must arrange before the goods leave China.

## The common thread: confirm before production

Every market above shares one pattern: the expensive mistakes happen when certification is discovered after the goods are made or shipped. Conformity certificates, local marks and pre-export verification are all origin-side tasks — they must be arranged before the container leaves Tianjin / Shanghai. The [shipping and warranty guide](/knowledge/battery-shipping-warranty/) covers the dangerous-goods paperwork (UN 2794 / UN 2800, Class 8, under the [IMDG Code](https://www.imo.org/)), and the [RFQ guide](/knowledge/oem-battery-rfq-guide/) shows how to make the destination market and its certification set an explicit RFQ field so the supplier quotes compliance, not just unit price.
