# Lead-acid battery dangerous-goods classification

Published by Dingwei Battery · Last reviewed 2026-09-10 · Sources: UN Model Regulations, IMO IMDG Code, US DOT PHMSA

Lead-acid batteries are dangerous goods — Class 8 corrosive, because the electrolyte is sulfuric acid. The classification is not optional paperwork; it decides your packaging, your labelling, your documentation and, if you get it wrong, whether the container is turned back at the port. Here is the classification, the test that changes the UN number, and how it compares with lithium.

**Short answer:** Lead-acid batteries ship as Class 8 corrosive dangerous goods under two UN numbers — UN 2794 (batteries, wet, filled with acid) for flooded starting batteries and UN 2800 (batteries, wet, non-spillable) for sealed designs that pass the vibration and pressure-differential tests. Sea freight follows the IMO IMDG Code, and the practical advantage over lithium is that lead-acid needs no UN 38.3 report.

## The two UN numbers

| UN number | Proper shipping name | Class | Applies to |
|---|---|---|---|
| UN 2794 | Batteries, wet, filled with acid | 8 | Flooded lead-acid batteries with free liquid electrolyte |
| UN 2800 | Batteries, wet, non-spillable | 8 | Sealed batteries that pass the non-spillable tests |
| UN 2796 | Battery fluid, acid | 8 | Sulfuric acid electrolyte shipped on its own |

The proper shipping names and numbers come from the \[UN Model Regulations\](https://unece.org/transport/dangerous-goods), maintained by UNECE, and are adopted into every national and modal rule set — the IMO \[IMDG Code\](https://www.imo.org/) for sea, US DOT 49 CFR for US road and rail, ADR for European road, and IATA DGR for air.

## What "non-spillable" (UN 2800) actually requires

UN 2800 status is not a marketing label — it is a tested claim. A battery qualifies as non-spillable only if it passes the vibration and pressure-differential tests specified in the transport regulations, proving the electrolyte will not escape under normal transport conditions. The practical consequence matters for freight cost and handling: a UN 2800 battery attracts lighter packaging and handling requirements than a UN 2794 flooded battery. The trap for buyers: a sealed battery without test records is legally still UN 2794, whatever the supplier's brochure says. See the \[US DOT PHMSA\](https://www.phmsa.dot.gov/) guidance for the authoritative US reading.

## Lead-acid vs lithium: different classes, different burdens

Buyers coming from lithium often carry assumptions that do not transfer. The two chemistries sit in different classes with different paperwork:

| Dimension | Lead-acid | Lithium-ion |
|---|---|---|
| UN numbers | UN 2794 / UN 2800 | UN 3480 (alone) / UN 3481 (with equipment) |
| Hazard class | Class 8 (corrosive) | Class 9 (miscellaneous) |
| UN 38.3 test | Not required | Required |
| Dominant risk | Acid spill, corrosion | Thermal runaway, fire |
| Documentation | Lower burden | Higher (test summaries, labelling, training) |

The lead-acid advantage is real but narrow: no UN 38.3 report. You still need Class 8 corrosive labelling, UN packaging where required, and a signed dangerous-goods declaration by a trained person. The lithium side — UN 3480/UN 3481 and the UN 38.3 requirement — is covered by IATA and the \[UN Model Regulations\](https://unece.org/transport/dangerous-goods); the comparison only matters here because it is the context most battery importers already know.

## What the class label and packaging require

-   **Class 8 corrosive label** on the package, and a Class 8 placard on the vehicle or container where regulations require it.
-   **UN-numbered marking** next to the proper shipping name.
-   **Compliant packaging** that contains acid and protects terminals from shorting — export-grade cartons and upright orientation.
-   **A dangerous-goods declaration** signed by a certified person, plus the MSDS for the specific product.

Misclassifying a battery as "general merchandise" is the single most common hazmat violation in the industry — and it is a fine, a delayed container or a refused shipment waiting to happen.

## Which UN number is your battery?

The classification is a decision, not a label, and it follows a short flow:

1.  **Is there free liquid electrolyte?** If the battery is flooded with acid that can spill, it is UN 2794.
2.  **Is it sealed and tested non-spillable?** If it passed the vibration and pressure-differential tests with records, it is UN 2800.
3.  **Sealed but untested?** Then it is still UN 2794 — a sealed case alone does not make a battery non-spillable.

The practical stakes are freight and handling: UN 2800 attracts lighter packaging and handling requirements, so a supplier who can document the test saves you money — and a supplier who cannot document it is selling a UN 2794 battery at a UN 2800 description. Ask for the test record, not the label.

## What the classification changes in practice

-   **Packaging** — UN 2794 needs packaging that contains acid and prevents shorting; UN 2800 is lighter but still needs Class 8 marking.
-   **Labelling and placarding** — the Class 8 corrosive label and the UN number travel with both.
-   **Documentation** — a dangerous-goods declaration signed by a certified person, plus the MSDS, for both.
-   **Air freight** — non-spillable UN 2800 is the only form generally considered for air; flooded UN 2794 is essentially sea and road freight.

The comparison that matters for importers is therefore not "dangerous or not" — both are dangerous goods — but "which UN number, and can the supplier prove it." That proof is the difference between a smooth clearance and a container turned back.
