# Battery terminal types and polarity

Published by Dingwei Battery · Last reviewed 2026-09-09 · Sources: SAE J537, EN 50342

A battery can match the right standard and case size and still not fit — because the terminal type, position or polarity is wrong. Terminal detail is a fitment decision, not an afterthought, and the conventions differ between markets.

**Short answer:** A battery with the right case size and capacity can still be the wrong battery if the terminal type, position or polarity is off. The three main designs are the tapered top post (most cars), the side terminal (some North American applications) and the stud/flag terminal (heavy-duty and marine). All three must be confirmed against the vehicle before ordering.

## Terminal type: three designs

Different markets and applications use different post designs:

| Type | Design | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Top post (tapered) | Round, slightly tapered posts on the lid | Most JIS/DIN/BCI passenger cars |
| Side terminal | Threaded sockets on the case side | Some North American GM and marine applications |
| Stud / flag | Threaded studs or flat flag lugs | Heavy-duty, marine, commercial |

A terminal that does not match the vehicle cable clamp will not connect securely — this is a physical compatibility issue, not a performance issue. Within the tapered top-post design, the SAE/DIN convention makes the positive post slightly larger than the negative (about 17.5 mm versus 15.9 mm) as a guard against reverse connection. JIS batteries use their own post sizes and are commonly available in "pencil" (small) or standard post variants — another reason a JIS battery is not a drop-in for a DIN tray.

## Terminal position: L and R

The same battery model can ship with the positive post on the left or the right. Manufacturers often denote this with an L or R suffix (for example, a D23L versus a D23R). The position matters because the vehicle’s cable lengths are fixed — the positive cable is routed to one side, and the battery must present the positive post there.

## Polarity: positive, negative, and ground

Polarity is the assignment of positive and negative to the two posts. Modern road vehicles are negative-ground — the negative terminal connects to the chassis. Reversing polarity on connection can damage the vehicle’s electrical system, so polarity is confirmed against the vehicle wiring before fitment.

## The reverse-connection risk

This is the failure mode a spec sheet cannot show you. A correct battery with reversed polarity is still a wrong battery, and the damage is real: current flows backwards through the vehicle’s electronics, regulators and diodes. The physical size difference between posts is a guard, not a guarantee — side-terminal and stud designs do not have it, and a determined or careless connection can still bridge the terminals. This is exactly why terminal detail is confirmed, not assumed, at the quoting stage.

## Post dimensions by standard

The tapered top post is not one size — the diameters differ by market, which is why a JIS battery’s clamps do not always seat on a DIN battery’s posts:

| Market | Positive post (typical) | Negative post (typical) | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| SAE / DIN | ~17.5 mm | ~15.9 mm | Positive larger as a reverse-connection guard |
| JIS (standard post) | Similar diameter | Same as positive | Posts often equal; check the variant |
| JIS (pencil post) | Smaller | Smaller | Narrow post for compact applications |

Post diameters are typical references, not universal — confirm the exact post size and the matching cable clamp for the specific model, because a clamp sized for one market does not always grip another.

## How to determine polarity on an unmarked battery

If the polarity is not marked, there are three reliable ways to read it:

1.  **Markings and colour** — the positive terminal is usually marked with a + symbol and often a red cap or red cable; the negative with a − symbol and black. The marks are on the case next to the posts.
2.  **Post size** — on SAE/DIN tapered posts, the positive post is physically larger. If the two posts are visibly different sizes, the larger one is positive.
3.  **Confirm with a meter** — a voltmeter across the terminals shows a positive reading when the red probe is on the positive post and black on negative. This is the definitive check, and it is worth the ten seconds before connecting anything.

In a modern negative-ground vehicle the negative terminal is the one that connects to the chassis, so tracing the negative cable to its body connection also identifies the polarity — but the voltmeter is faster and cannot be fooled by an aftermarket cable colour.

## Terminal care and the corrosion cycle

Terminal corrosion is the most common connection problem, and it has a known cause: acid vapour escaping around the posts reacts with the metal clamps to form a white or blue-green deposit that adds resistance. The result is a battery that tests healthy but will not pass enough current to start. For a manufacturer, the countermeasure is a sealed, maintenance-free design and a tight post-to-cover seal; for the buyer, it is confirming the battery is genuinely maintenance-free rather than a vented design labelled as one.

## Adapters and aftermarket terminals

In the aftermarket, terminal adapters and replacement clamps are common — a sleeve to step a post up or down, a stud-to-post converter, or a universal clamp. They exist because markets genuinely differ, and a distributor often carries one battery and adapts it to several vehicles. The caveat: an adapter adds a second mechanical and electrical joint, and every joint is a point of resistance and corrosion. For a clean OEM program the terminal should be specified correctly in the first place, not adapted after the fact. Adapters are a repair-market tool, not an OEM solution.

## Why these are confirmed on inquiry

On Dingwei reference pages, terminal type and polarity are marked "confirm on inquiry" rather than guessed. That is deliberate: a wrong terminal specification turns a correct battery into a wrong battery. The manufacturer confirms terminal detail against the buyer’s vehicle or fleet requirement before production.

## What buyers should ask

-   What terminal type does the vehicle use — top post, side terminal or stud?
-   Is the positive post on the left or right (L/R)?
-   What is the correct polarity for the vehicle wiring?
-   Does the heavy-duty application need dual or flag terminals?
