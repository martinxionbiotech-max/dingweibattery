# Battery storage and shelf life

Published by Dingwei Battery · Last reviewed 2026-09-10 · Sources: Battery University (BU-403, BU-802b), Power Sonic sulfation guidance

A lead-acid battery on a shelf is not a static asset — it is a slowly discharging one. Get storage wrong and you ship batteries that are already partially sulfated, which is a warranty claim and a brand hit disguised as a logistics detail. This page turns the chemistry into a warehouse procedure: how fast batteries discharge, how often to top them up, and why rotation order decides how much warranty you keep.

**Short answer:** Lead-acid batteries self-discharge at roughly 3–5% per month at moderate temperature, and the rate doubles for every 10°C rise. The practical rules: store cool (below 24°C), keep batteries fully charged, apply a top-up charge every 6 months, and rotate stock first-in-first-out — because a battery that sits a year has both consumed its warranty clock and begun sulfating below 50% charge.

## The self-discharge curve, in numbers

Self-discharge is the steady loss of charge a battery suffers even when nothing is connected. For lead-acid the typical rate is about 3–5% per month at room temperature (see \[Battery University's self-discharge data\](https://batteryuniversity.com/article/bu-802b-what-does-elevated-self-discharge-do)), and the rate is temperature-driven: it roughly doubles for every 10°C of temperature rise. A battery losing 4% per month at 20°C can lose 8–10% per month at 30°C — which is why a warehouse in a hot climate ages stock dramatically faster than the same stock in a cool one.

## Why "below 50% charge" is the line you must not cross

Self-discharge is not just a lost charge — below about 50% state of charge, **sulfation** begins: lead sulfate crystals harden on the plates and stop converting back during recharge. That capacity loss is permanent, and it is the mechanism that turns an old shelf battery into a warranty claim. The same chemistry explains why the storage rule is "fully charged, cool, and topped up" — a full charge keeps the battery above the sulfation threshold, and cool storage slows the march toward it (see \[Power Sonic's sulfation guidance\](https://www.power-sonic.com/what-is-a-sulfated-battery-and-how-do-you-prevent-it)).

## The warehouse procedure, distilled

1.  **Store fully charged** — never warehouse a battery that arrived discharged or partially charged.
2.  **Store cool** — below about 24°C (75°F) where practical; keep pallets out of direct sun.
3.  **Top up every 6 months** — apply a maintenance charge and mark the date on the pallet; sealed-battery suppliers often tighten this to 3–6 months.
4.  **Rotate FIFO** — first-in, first-out, so the oldest pallet ships first.
5.  **Check voltage before dispatch** — a quick voltage reading catches a battery that has drifted below the safe threshold.

The rule of thumb to remember: **6 months** is the practical safe window without a top-up charge. Past that, you are spending warranty and capacity with every extra week on the shelf.

## Why storage and warranty are the same decision

The warranty clock starts at the bill of lading date — which means a battery that sits 6 months in your warehouse has already consumed half its 12-month warranty before a customer touches it. Stack that on top of the self-discharge, and a slow-moving pallet is a double liability: less warranty to offer, and a higher chance the battery fails inside the window that remains. FIFO is not warehouse bureaucracy — it is how you keep the warranty you sold. The claim-side detail is in the [warranty claim process](/knowledge/battery-warranty-claim-process/) page.

## The storage-cost calculation

Quantify the risk to see why the procedure matters. A battery self-discharging at 5% per month that sits 12 months untended has lost most of its charge and crossed deep into sulfation territory — a warranty liability, not an asset. The cost of a quarterly top-up pass across a warehouse is a rounding error against the cost of one container that arrives partially sulfated and triggers claims. The maintenance charge is the cheapest insurance in the whole logistics chain.

## Temperature thresholds at a glance

| Storage condition | Effect on self-discharge | Practical consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Below 24°C (75°F) | Baseline — roughly 3–5% per month | 6-month top-up schedule holds |
| 30°C and above | Roughly doubles (8–10% per month) | Shorten top-up to every 3 months |
| Below about 50% charge | Sulfation begins to harden | Capacity loss becomes permanent |
