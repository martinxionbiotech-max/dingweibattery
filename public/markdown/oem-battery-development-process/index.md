# The OEM battery development process

Published by Dingwei Battery · Last reviewed 2026-09-10 · Source: Chengguang Power Tech Co., Ltd. technical documentation

OEM development is a gated process — seven stages, each ending in a milestone and, at four of them, a buyer sign-off. Understanding the sequence tells you where to be involved and where to insist on written approval, which is the difference between a battery that stays on spec and one that drifts.

**Short answer:** OEM battery development moves through seven gates — consultation, specification, design and branding, sampling, mass production, quality inspection and shipping — over a 60–90 day cycle. The buyer signs four of them (specification, sample, quality standard, shipment), and the key milestone is the approved pre-production sample, which locks the specification and releases mass production.

## The seven stages and their milestones

| Stage | Milestone | Typical cycle | Buyer sign-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1\. Consultation | Feasibility confirmation | 2–3 days | — |
| 2\. Specification | Signed spec sheet | 3–5 days | Yes |
| 3\. Design & branding | Approved artwork | 3–7 days | Yes |
| 4\. Sampling | Approved pre-production sample | 10–20 days | Yes |
| 5\. Mass production | Container-load batch | 30–45 days | — |
| 6\. Quality inspection | Signed test data | 2–4 days | Yes |
| 7\. Shipping | Bill of lading | 3–5 weeks | Yes |

The cycle times above are planning figures for a standard model on an existing platform; a custom case or a new terminal layout lengthens sampling and tooling. The full 60–90 day picture is in the [OEM manufacturing guide](/knowledge/oem-battery-guide/).

## The four gates that need your signature

Not every stage needs your approval — four do, and they are the ones where the outcome is decided:

1.  **Specification** — sign every parameter, including the CCA test basis.
2.  **Sample** — test the pre-production sample against the spec before approving.
3.  **Quality standard** — agree the acceptance criteria before mass production begins.
4.  **Shipment** — confirm the documents before release.

If the manufacturer signs all four on your behalf, you have given away control of the outcome. Keep the sequence tied to the [program phases](/knowledge/how-oem-battery-programs-work/) so both sides know where approval is required — and note that the quality-standard gate is a defined set of acceptance criteria, the same discipline the \[ISO 9001\](https://www.iso.org/iso-9001-quality-management.html) quality-management framework requires for any repeatable process.

## From sample to mass production: the critical handoff

The most consequential transition is between stage 4 (sampling) and stage 5 (mass production). The approved sample becomes the **golden sample** — the physical reference every later batch is checked against. Three disciplines make the handoff safe:

1.  **Approve on the tested sample, not the photo** — ask for the CCA and capacity report of the actual sample, on the named test basis.
2.  **Lock the sample in the contract** — reference its serial number and test report in the purchase order.
3.  **Keep one yourself** — you cannot adjudicate a future claim against a sample you never held.

A factory that rushes you past sampling into production is usually hiding the gap between its sample line and its production line. This is the single most common — and most expensive — development failure, and it is entirely preventable.

## Where buyers should be involved

-   **Specification** — sign off every parameter.
-   **Sampling** — test before approving.
-   **Quality inspection** — agree the acceptance criteria.
-   **Shipment** — confirm the documentation.

Everything between those gates is the manufacturer's job. Staying out of the way on production while holding the four gates is the discipline that produces a repeatable battery. See the [factory audit](/knowledge/how-audit-battery-factory/) guide for what "process control" should look like on the line, and the [specification guide](/knowledge/oem-battery-specification-guide/) for writing stage 2 correctly.

## Why gates matter more than speed

A skipped gate is how specification drift happens. A slower, gated process protects the repeatability that makes an OEM program profitable — and the cost of a skipped gate is measured in containers, not days. The 10–20 days spent on sampling is the cheapest insurance in the whole program, and the whole gated sequence is the practical shape of the defect-prevention discipline that \[IATF 16949\](https://www.iatfglobaloversight.org/) codifies for automotive manufacturing.
