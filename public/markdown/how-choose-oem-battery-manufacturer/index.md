# How to choose an OEM battery manufacturer

Published by Dingwei Battery · Last reviewed 2026-09-10 · Source: Chengguang Power Tech Co., Ltd. technical documentation; IATF 16949

Choose a manufacturer that can match your standard, hold quality systems, and repeat your specification order after order. The method below weights the five factors that actually predict that — and lists the traps that make a weak supplier look strong.

**Short answer:** The single most important factor in choosing an OEM battery manufacturer is repeatability — whether the 10th container matches the first approved sample. Score every candidate on five weighted dimensions: specification coverage (25%), certifications (20%), scale (20%), repeatability (20%) and export experience (15%). A score below 3.5 out of 5 overall, or below 3 on repeatability, removes a candidate regardless of price.

## 1\. Match the standard family

Your market determines the standard: JIS, DIN, BCI, AS or SA. Confirm the factory regularly produces that family rather than treating your order as a one-off. A factory that runs JIS daily has the moulds, the test fixtures and the CCA measurement habits for your market; one that has “done a few DIN orders” is learning on your container. Lock the detail in the [specifications reference](/knowledge/battery-specifications-guide/).

## 2\. Check quality systems

Ask which certifications the manufacturer holds, and — this is the part most buyers skip — ask for the certificate _number_ so you can confirm it is current. For automotive supply, [IATF 16949](https://www.iatfglobaloversight.org/) is the relevant quality system; ISO 9001 covers general quality management. An IATF certificate runs a 3-year cycle with surveillance audits, so the paper date alone does not prove standing. Chengguang Power Tech Co., Ltd. holds IATF 16949, ISO 9001, ISO 45001, OHSAS 18001 and can supply the numbers on request.

## 3\. Verify factory capability

Look at plant size, line count, daily output, export experience and whether the facility can support your volume. Chengguang Power Tech Co., Ltd. operates a 200,000 m² facility with 18 automated lines, producing 40,000 batteries per day and exporting to 70+ countries.

**Capacity fit, calculated.** Capability is not just “big enough” — it is about where your order sits in their queue. If your annual need is 50,000 batteries and the plant makes 40,000 a day, your whole year is under 2 production days. That means two things: you are not their biggest customer, and — more importantly — you may be their most interruptible one when a larger order lands. The ideal fit is a factory big enough to absorb your volume without strain, but small enough that your order matters. Read the on-site method in the [factory audit guide](/knowledge/how-audit-battery-factory/).

## 4\. Define the specification before quoting

Fix voltage, capacity, CCA, case size, terminal and polarity first. Ambiguous specifications produce samples that cannot be fairly compared. See the [OEM specification guide](/knowledge/oem-battery-specification-guide/) and the [specifications reference](/knowledge/battery-specifications-guide/).

## The five-point decision framework, weighted

Do not choose on one number. Score every shortlisted manufacturer against the same five factors, weight them, and total. This is the framework the sourcing pillar references, expanded to a usable scorecard.

| Factor | Weight | What to score | Source of the score |
|---|---|---|---|
| Specification coverage | 25% | Can they produce the standard family and models you need in-house? | RFQ response + model list |
| Certifications | 20% | Are current IATF 16949 / ISO 9001 certificates verifiable by number? | Certifier register |
| Scale | 20% | Does daily output map to your annual volume without making you the smallest customer? | Factory audit |
| Repeatability | 20% | Is there a locked specification and a written change-control process? | Contract + sample history |
| Export experience | 15% | Which markets do they serve, and can they document them? | References + documents |

Score each factor 1–5, multiply by the weight, and sum. The thresholds that matter: below 3.5 overall, remove the candidate; below 3 on repeatability, remove the candidate no matter how cheap. A supplier that scores 5 on price and 1 on repeatability is the most expensive supplier on your list — it just bills you later, in warranty claims and rework.

## Questions that expose a weak supplier

-   They cannot name the CCA test method.
-   They quote before confirming the standard family.
-   They cannot show a sample from a recent production run.
-   They will not share the certificate number for verification.
-   They cannot name their plate curing window.

Run each shortlisted factory through the [OEM battery supplier checklist](/knowledge/oem-battery-supplier-checklist/) before you commit volume, and verify the entity itself first — see the [supplier verification](/knowledge/battery-supplier-verification/) guide.

## Traps that make a weak supplier look strong

-   **The brochure factory** — glossy photos of a line the company does not own. Verify the business licence and a live walkthrough, not the photo set.
-   **The trading-company discount** — a “factory-direct” price that is actually a trader’s 5–15% markup on someone else’s plant, with no quality control to match.
-   **The expired certificate** — a certificate image that is within its printed dates but suspended. The number is what you check.
-   **The one-sample wonder** — a flawless sample followed by a tenth container that is not the same battery, because there was no change control.

## Red and green flag signals

| Signal | Red flag | Green flag |
|---|---|---|
| Quoting | Prices before the standard and CCA basis are fixed | Repeats your specification back before quoting |
| Certificates | “We have all certifications” with no numbers | Certificate numbers offered for verification |
| Capacity | Output claim the formation area cannot support | Casting, formation and headcount that agree |
| Change control | No written process for specification changes | A documented ECO process and locked sample |
| References | No bill of lading or customer reference offered | Names real destination markets and can document them |

## Lead-acid-specific checks

For lead-acid, the highest-risk checks are process checks, not marketing checks:

-   **Grid alloy** — maintenance-free batteries normally use lead-calcium grids; confirm the alloy is stated.
-   **Paste consistency** — ask how paste is controlled between batches.
-   **Curing** — verify the plate curing window is sufficient, normally 2–4 days.
-   **Formation** — confirm the battery is fully formed and 100% end-of-line tested.

These are the same checks a buyer should walk through on a [lead-acid factory audit](/knowledge/how-audit-battery-factory/).

## The price you are not quoted

Unit price is not the whole cost. The real cost of a manufacturer is the unit price plus the cost of its mistakes: warranty returns, rework, late shipments and the lost sales when a container arrives wrong. A supplier that is 5% cheaper on paper and drifts on specification costs far more than 5% in the field. This is why repeatability carries a 20% weight — it is the discount you never see on the invoice.
