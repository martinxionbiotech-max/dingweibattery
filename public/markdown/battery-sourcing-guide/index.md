# How to source OEM batteries from China: the complete guide

Published by Dingwei Battery · Last reviewed 2026-09-10 · Sources: IATF 16949, USTR Section 301, EU Batteries Regulation (EU) 2023/1542, IMDG Code

Sourcing OEM batteries from China is not one decision — it is seven, and they happen in a fixed order. Get the order right and the process is mechanical; get it wrong and you lose a container’s margin to a supplier that never should have been shortlisted. This guide walks the sequence from specification to landed goods, gives you a weighted scorecard for choosing a factory, and ends with the parts suppliers would rather you skip: how to verify a factory is real, and what the landed-cost math actually looks like.

**Short answer:** Source OEM batteries in seven stages — fix the specification, build a shortlist, verify the suppliers, audit the factory, issue a clean RFQ, confirm MOQ and lead time, and clear import compliance — in that order. The single highest-value step is verification: a trading company posing as a factory typically adds 5–15% and removes your quality control, so check the business licence before you pay. A first order is usually one 20ft container (600–800 units), lead time runs 30–45 days, and the whole cycle from RFQ to landed goods takes 60–90 days.

## The seven-stage sequence

Every sourcing failure traces back to doing a later stage before an earlier one — quoting before the specification is fixed, auditing before the shortlist is credible, or negotiating MOQ before the supplier is verified. The stages are:

1.  **Specify** — fix standard family, case group, Ah, CCA basis, terminal and technology.
2.  **Shortlist** — pick 3–5 candidates from the right industry regions.
3.  **Verify** — confirm each is a real factory with current certificates.
4.  **Audit** — confirm process control and repeatability, on site or by third party.
5.  **RFQ** — issue one complete scope so quotes are comparable.
6.  **Commit** — confirm MOQ, lead time, payment and warranty.
7.  **Import** — clear tariffs, certifications and dangerous-goods shipping.

Skip one stage and you pay for it later. A supplier you did not verify will surface a problem exactly when it is most expensive to fix — after the deposit is paid.

## The decision tree: what to ask at each stage

Each stage has one question that matters more than the rest. Ask it, get a verifiable answer, then move on.

| Stage | The one question | What a good answer looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1\. Specify | Which standard family and CCA test basis? | “JIS D26, 72 Ah, 490 CCA at −15°C.” |
| 2\. Shortlist | Which region and what is your real output? | Named plant location + daily output you can verify. |
| 3\. Verify | Show me your business licence and current IATF certificate. | Documents that check out against official registers. |
| 4\. Audit | What is your plate curing window? | A stated window (typically 2–4 days) plus batch records. |
| 5\. RFQ | Quote one scope, one model per line. | A line-item quote that repeats your specification back. |
| 6\. Commit | What is the MOQ logic and lead time breakdown? | Container-based MOQ + production/shipping split. |
| 7\. Import | Which certifications does my destination market require? | A named certificate set for your market. |

## Stage 1 — Fix the specification first

A battery that is not fully specified cannot be quoted, compared or audited. The five parts — standard family (JIS / DIN-EN / BCI / AS / SA), case group, capacity at C20, CCA on a named test basis, and terminal type plus polarity — are one decision, not five separate ones. A supplier that quotes “a 72 Ah battery” without asking the standard is guessing, and so is the price.

The full treatment lives in the specifications cluster — start with the [automotive battery specifications reference](/knowledge/battery-specifications-guide/) and lock the model against the [group size chart](/knowledge/battery-group-size-chart/) before you contact anyone.

## Stage 2 — Build a shortlist from the right regions

China’s lead-acid battery production is clustered, not spread evenly. Different regions specialise in different products and price points, and a buyer who shortlists by region gets a tighter set of candidates than one who shortlists by search ranking. The main clusters — Hebei (where Chengguang Power Tech is based, in Jinzhou), Shandong, Jiangsu, Zhejiang and Anhui — differ in process strength, export experience and cost structure. Read the [China battery industry regions](/knowledge/china-battery-industry-regions/) guide before you build the list.

Keep the shortlist to 3–5 factories. More than that and the RFQ comparison becomes unmanageable; fewer than three and you have no competitive reference.

## Stage 3 — Verify the supplier is what it claims

This is the stage buyers skip, and it is the most expensive stage to skip. Three things must be true before a factory earns a spot on your list:

-   **It is a real legal entity** — check the business licence and unified social credit code against the National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System ([gsxt.gov.cn](https://www.gsxt.gov.cn/index.html)).
-   **It is a factory, not a trading company** — the licence lists the business scope; “production” (生产制造) versus “trading” (贸易) is written on it.
-   **Its certificates are current** — an IATF 16949 certificate has a validity period and a scope; ask for the certificate number and confirm it against the certifier’s register.

The full method — including how to test whether claimed capacity is real — is in the [supplier verification](/knowledge/battery-supplier-verification/) guide. The short version: a supplier that will not show its licence before an order will show you nothing useful after it.

## Stage 4 — Audit the factory

Verification confirms the supplier is real; the audit confirms it can repeat your specification. Walk the line in the order the battery is made — grid casting, paste mixing, pasting, curing, assembly, formation, sealing, test — and at each step ask what is measured and how rejects are handled. The single most telling question is the curing window: a factory that rushes plate curing to save days ships batteries that fail early.

For lead-acid the audit is lighter than for lithium — no cell-safety or thermal-runaway review — which makes it cheap to do properly. See the [factory audit guide](/knowledge/how-audit-battery-factory/) for the full walkthrough, and run every candidate through the [supplier checklist](/knowledge/oem-battery-supplier-checklist/) so they are scored the same way.

## Stage 5 — Issue one complete RFQ

Most RFQs fail on three missing fields: the CCA test basis, the annual volume, and one standard per line. Without them, every supplier quotes a different scope and the prices are not comparable. Send the specification block, the volume block, the branding block and the commercial block as one document, one model per line. The [RFQ guide](/knowledge/oem-battery-rfq-guide/) has the template and the field checklist.

Do not put a price target in a first RFQ. Suppliers quote to a number when you give them one; quote on scope first and negotiate price second.

## Stage 6 — Confirm MOQ and lead time

Battery MOQ is a container-logic number, not a marketing number. A 20ft container holds roughly 33 m³ and about 28,000 kg of payload, but the practical load for starter batteries is set by pallet stacking and carton crush strength — which is why a first order lands around 600–800 units rather than the 1,400+ the raw weight limit would allow. Lead time of 30–45 days is the sum of production plus pre-shipment testing, and it is stable year-round because lead-acid has no harvest season. The [MOQ and lead time guide](/knowledge/oem-battery-moq-lead-time/) breaks both down.

## Stage 7 — Clear the import path

Before you commit a cent, know the destination market’s tariff, certification and environmental requirements. The US adds a 25% Section 301 surcharge on Chinese batteries on top of the base duty; the EU applies the [Batteries Regulation (EU) 2023/1542](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/1542/oj) and CE marking; Gulf states require SASO/GSO conformity; and lead-acid ships as Class 8 corrosive dangerous goods (UN 2794 / UN 2800) under the [IMDG Code](https://www.imo.org/). The [import regulations guide](/knowledge/battery-import-regulations/) maps the requirements market by market, and the [shipping and warranty guide](/knowledge/battery-shipping-warranty/) covers the dangerous-goods paperwork.

## The weighted supplier scorecard

Score every shortlisted factory against the same five dimensions. The weights below are a starting point — raise the repeatability weight if you plan a multi-year program, and raise the certification weight if your market requires IATF 16949 or SASO.

| Dimension | Weight | What you are scoring | Where to find the data |
|---|---|---|---|
| Specification coverage | 25% | Can they produce your standard family and models in-house? | RFQ response + model list |
| Certifications | 20% | Are current IATF 16949 / ISO 9001 / market certs verifiable? | Certificate register |
| Scale & capacity | 20% | Does daily output map to your annual volume without strain? | Factory audit |
| Repeatability | 20% | Is there a locked specification and change control? | Contract + sample history |
| Export experience | 15% | Which markets do they serve, and can they document them? | Reference + docs |

Score each dimension 1–5, multiply by the weight, and total. A score below 3.5 overall, or below 3 on repeatability, should remove a candidate regardless of price. Chengguang Power Tech Co., Ltd. holds IATF 16949, ISO 9001, ISO 45001, OHSAS 18001, operates 18 automated lines producing 40,000 batteries per day, and exports to 70+ countries — so it can be scored with exactly this card. Run the detailed method in the [how to choose a manufacturer](/knowledge/how-choose-oem-battery-manufacturer/) guide.

## Two calculations that reframe the decision

**The audit-cost ratio.** A one-day third-party audit in China costs roughly US$1,500–3,000. A first container of 700 batteries at a US$40 landed unit cost is US$28,000; a 12-month program at one container a month is over US$300,000. The audit is therefore under 1% of a single container and a rounding error against a year. Paying US$3,000 to avoid a specification drift that ruins a US$28,000 shipment is not a cost — it is the cheapest line item in the whole program.

**The trading-company margin.** A trading company that fronts as a factory typically adds 5–15% to the ex-factory price and removes your visibility into quality. On a US$28,000 container that is US$1,400–4,200 per shipment — more than the audit costs — and you have traded it for a supplier you cannot hold accountable on process. Verify before you pay, and the margin question answers itself.

## What suppliers will not volunteer

-   **“Factory” photos can be bought.** A trading company can photograph someone else’s line. The licence and a live walkthrough, not a photo set, establish who owns the equipment.
-   **Certificates can be expired or suspended.** A certificate is a point-in-time document. Ask for the number and check the certifier’s register — a suspended IATF certificate is not worth the paper.
-   **A low unit price hides a hidden cost.** A quote that skips the CCA basis, tooling or packaging is not a cheaper battery — it is a smaller scope. Compare on scope, not on the bare number.
-   **MOQ reflects the supplier’s inventory pressure, not yours.** A supplier that pushes a huge MOQ may be offloading stock, not protecting your unit cost. Ask for the MOQ logic.

## Common mistakes that cost a container

1.  **Quoting before specifying** — you collect prices for three different batteries and call it a comparison.
2.  **Skipping verification** — the cheapest stage to skip and the most expensive mistake to discover.
3.  **Buying on price alone** — a 5% cheaper quote that drifts on specification costs more than it saves.
4.  **One model per RFQ line, ignored** — mixed lines force a guess and a range instead of a quote.
5.  **Discovering import duties after ordering** — a 25% surcharge found late can wipe the entire margin.
