# How OEM battery programs work

Published by Dingwei Battery · Last reviewed 2026-09-10 · Source: Chengguang Power Tech Co., Ltd. technical documentation

An OEM battery program is a repeatable supply arrangement, not a one-off order. The point of the structure is simple: make the tenth container match the first approved sample. That repeatability is what the program buys you — and it is what a loose one-off order cannot.

**Short answer:** An OEM battery program runs in 7 stages — consultation, specification, design and branding, sampling, mass production, quality inspection and shipping — but you only sign off 4 of them: specification, sample, quality standard and shipment. The mechanism that makes the whole thing safe is the specification lock: an approved sample plus written parameters plus change control, so repeat orders match the first.

## The seven-stage flow

The program follows a fixed sequence, and each stage has one deliverable and one checkpoint. Skipping a stage is how a specification drifts.

| Stage | Manufacturer delivers | Buyer action |
|---|---|---|
| 1\. Consultation | Feasibility view on the requested spec | Confirm vehicle, market and volume intent |
| 2\. Specification | Written spec sheet, every parameter | Sign every line, including the CCA test basis |
| 3\. Design & branding | Label, case and packaging artwork | Approve artwork and documentation |
| 4\. Sampling | Pre-production sample + test report | Test the physical sample before approving |
| 5\. Mass production | Scheduled production, in-process control | Confirm schedule and any change freeze |
| 6\. Quality inspection | Inspection and test data | Agree acceptance criteria before release |
| 7\. Shipping | Export packaging and documents | Confirm documents before shipment |

## The specification lock

The core of an OEM program is not the first order; it is the locked specification that makes the tenth order match the first approved sample. The lock has three parts:

1.  **The approved sample** — a physical unit you tested and accepted, referenced by serial number in the contract.
2.  **The written parameters** — every spec line, including the CCA test basis, signed.
3.  **Change control** — a rule that any change to material, alloy or process requires your written approval.

Without a lock, a repeat order becomes a new development project with fresh approval risk. The [development process](/knowledge/oem-battery-development-process/) page shows where each gate must be signed before the lock is released to production.

## Production and quality: what the factory controls

Once the sample is approved, the specification goes to the line. Chengguang Power Tech Co., Ltd. runs 18 automated lines with \[IATF 16949\](https://www.iatfglobaloversight.org/) process control and 100% end-of-line testing — the automotive quality standard that makes container-to-container consistency possible, not just hoped for. What to look for on the line is covered in the [quality control](/knowledge/battery-quality-control/) page, and the economic reason lead-acid is worth sourcing this way — near-total recyclability and a dominant-material cost base — is documented by \[Battery Council International\](https://batterycouncil.org/sources).

## Repeat orders: the payoff

The first order carries the development time — specification, sampling and approval. Repeat orders collapse to production plus shipping (30–45 days after order confirmation), because the specification is already locked. That is the economics of the program: the setup cost is amortised across every subsequent container, and the consistency risk is retired by the lock. This is why a program beats a string of one-off purchases.

## What can go wrong, and where

-   **Skipped sampling** — approving a drawing but not the physical sample is the single most common failure, and it surfaces as a repeat order that differs from the first.
-   **Unsigned CCA basis** — if the test method (JIS −15°C vs EN −18°C) is not written down, a later batch can "meet spec" on a different, easier test.
-   **No change control** — a factory that switches alloy or separator to save cost has no obligation to tell you unless the contract says so.
