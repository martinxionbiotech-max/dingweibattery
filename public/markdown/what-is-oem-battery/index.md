# What is an OEM battery?

Published by Dingwei Battery · Last reviewed 2026-09-10 · Source: Chengguang Power Tech Co., Ltd. technical documentation

An OEM battery is a battery produced to the buyer’s specification and sold under the buyer’s brand — rather than the factory’s own label. The single sentence that matters is the one about ownership: you own the design and the brand, the factory owns the manufacturing.

**Short answer:** An OEM battery is built to your specification — voltage, Ah, CCA, terminal and label — and sold under your brand — a program that runs from RFQ to first container in 60–90 days. Quality is set by the specification you sign, not the label: the same factory often runs branded and OEM batteries on the same line, so the difference is whose specification and quality gate controls the output.

## The core idea

OEM stands for original equipment manufacturer. In battery sourcing the term is used in reverse of how the car industry uses it: _you_ are the buyer who owns the product definition and brand, and the factory is the original equipment manufacturer that produces to your agreed specification — voltage, capacity, CCA, standard family, case size, terminal layout and labelling.

## OEM vs ODM vs white label: the ownership split

All three arrangements can look identical on a shelf — the difference is who owns the design, who owns the brand, and how much development risk you carry.

| Model | Who owns design | Who owns brand | Typical buyer |
|---|---|---|---|
| OEM | Buyer | Buyer | Buyer has a defined specification |
| ODM | Manufacturer | Buyer | Buyer wants a proven platform |
| White label | Manufacturer | Buyer | Buyer wants the fastest market entry |

**OEM** means you bring the specification and the manufacturer builds to it — you own the design. **ODM** means the manufacturer offers an existing design you can brand and adjust — they own the design, you own the brand. **White label** is the shallowest form: you apply your identity to an off-the-shelf product that any competitor can also buy. The deeper your ownership, the more control and the more development work. ## What goes into an OEM battery specification

At minimum, an OEM battery project defines the standard family (JIS, DIN, BCI, AS or SA), voltage, capacity at C20 (Ah), CCA on a named test basis, dimensions, terminal type and polarity. Reference models such as 65D26 or H6 are often used as the starting point, then adjusted. The [OEM specification guide](/knowledge/oem-battery-specification-guide/) walks each parameter.

## The advantages of OEM supply

-   **Exclusivity** — a product no competitor can buy off the shelf, because the specification is yours.
-   **Consistency** — a locked specification means the tenth container matches the first approved sample.
-   **Cost control** — you buy factory-direct capability, not a distributor's margin.
-   **Brand building** — the label, packaging and product are yours, so the equity accrues to you.

## The risks, stated plainly

-   **Specification drift** — a repeat order that comes back different from what you approved. This is the classic failure mode, and it is prevented by a locked spec and a tested sample, not by trust.
-   **Development effort** — OEM puts the definition work on you. If you cannot specify CCA basis, terminal and group size precisely, you will pay for the factory to guess.
-   **Minimums** — a first order is typically one 20ft container (600–800 units), because weight caps the load before volume.
-   **Lead-price exposure** — lead is 75–80% of the material cost, so your price moves with the LME unless you negotiate a fixed-price clause.

## Why lead-acid OEM is a different game from lithium

Lead-acid is a 150-year-old supply chain with a mature, standardised product. Unlike lithium cells (Class 9), lead-acid starting batteries ship as Class 8 corrosive dangerous goods (UN 2794 / UN 2800) but need no UN 38.3 test report, so compliance is lighter. The chemistry is also a commodity: lead dominates the cost, so the factory's real value-add is process control and repeatability, not exotic technology — and lead's near-total recyclability is part of why the chemistry stays economically rational (see \[Battery Council International's lead battery facts\](https://batterycouncil.org/sources)).
