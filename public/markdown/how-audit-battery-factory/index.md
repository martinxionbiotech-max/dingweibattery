# How to audit a lead-acid battery factory

Published by Dingwei Battery · Last reviewed 2026-09-10 · Source: Chengguang Power Tech Co., Ltd. technical documentation; IATF 16949

A factory audit is how you verify that a battery manufacturer can deliver your specification repeatably — not just once. Walk the line in production order, ask one question per stage, and check the documents against what you see.

**Short answer:** Audit a battery factory in production order — grid casting, paste mixing, pasting, curing, assembly, formation, sealing and test — and at each stage ask what is measured and how rejects are handled. The most telling single question is curing time: a factory that rushes curing to save days ships weak plates that fail early, so ask for the curing window (typically 2–4 days). A one-day third-party audit costs US$1,500–3,000, under 1% of a first container.

## Before the visit: the document set

Half the audit happens before you walk the line. Request these documents and read them first — they tell you what the factory _claims_, which the walkthrough then confirms or contradicts:

-   **Certificates** — IATF 16949 / ISO 9001 numbers and scopes, to verify as current. Chengguang Power Tech Co., Ltd. holds IATF 16949, ISO 9001, ISO 45001, OHSAS 18001.
-   **Process flow** — a written production flow with control points at each stage.
-   **Control plan and FMEA** — the automotive-style documents that show a factory thinks in failure modes, not just throughput.
-   **Batch records** — a sample batch traced from raw material to finished battery.
-   **Test procedures** — the CCA and capacity methods, with the test basis named.

A factory that has an IATF 16949 system should produce these on request. One that hesitates on the control plan is telling you it runs on habit, not on a documented system.

## The ten stages to walk, in production order

Walk the factory in the order the battery is made. For each stage, ask what is measured and how rejects are controlled:

1.  **Grid casting** — check grid thickness and alloy consistency; ask what happens to a grid outside tolerance.
2.  **Paste mixing** — check mix control and batch records; ask how paste density is held between batches.
3.  **Pasting** — check plate weight and paste application; ask how plate weight is verified.
4.  **Curing** — check time and conditions; a typical window is 2–4 days. This is the stage to be strictest on — rushed curing is the classic shortcut.
5.  **Assembly** — check plate alignment and separator handling.
6.  **Welding** — check inter-cell and terminal welds; a poor weld is a hidden early failure.
7.  **Electrolyte filling** — check acid volume and density control.
8.  **Formation charging** — check the charging profile and duration; formation is usually the capacity bottleneck.
9.  **Sealing** — check lid, vent and leak control.
10.  **Testing** — confirm 100% end-of-line electrical test plus CCA and capacity sampling.

Take this sequence with you, and compare it to the documented checks on the [OEM battery supplier checklist](/knowledge/oem-battery-supplier-checklist/).

## IATF 16949 review points

If the factory claims [IATF 16949](https://www.iatfglobaloversight.org/), three specific things should be visible that a non-certified plant usually lacks:

-   **Control plan at each process step** — not just a QC desk at the end, but defined controls at casting, pasting, curing and formation.
-   **Change management** — a written engineering change (ECO) process so a specification change is approved, not improvised.
-   **Traceability** — a batch that maps back to raw-material lots and forward to finished product, which is what makes recall and warranty adjudication possible.

Ask to see all three on the walkthrough. An IATF certificate without a visible control plan and change process is a wall decoration, not a system.

## The audit-cost calculation

The arithmetic that should end any hesitation about auditing:

Audit cost ÷ order value = a fraction of one percent.

A one-day third-party audit in China costs roughly US$1,500–3,000. A first container of 700 batteries at a US$40 landed unit cost is US$28,000. A 12-month program at one container a month exceeds US$300,000. The audit is under 1% of a single container — and it is the only line item in the program that _prevents_ a much larger loss. A specification drift discovered after the deposit is paid costs a container, not an audit fee.

## What to bring home from an audit

-   Current certificates, with numbers you can verify.
-   Sample of a recent production run.
-   A specification sheet for your reference model.
-   Agreed commercial terms, in writing.
-   The control plan and change process, if you are doing a long program.

## Lead-acid audit is simpler than lithium

Lead-acid audit effort concentrates on process consistency and raw materials. A lithium-cell audit adds cell safety and thermal-runaway controls; lead-acid does not carry the same cell-safety audit burden. That makes the lead-acid audit shorter and easier to standardise — which in turn makes it cheap enough that skipping it is the expensive choice.
